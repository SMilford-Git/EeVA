About EeVA

Ethical evaluation without pretending to solve ethics

EeVA is designed as a practical aid for ethical reflection. It does not claim to produce the one correct moral answer. Instead, it helps users examine a proposed use case through several ethical lenses so that assumptions, tensions, trade-offs, and unanswered questions become clearer.

EeVA, the Ethical eValuation Agent, emerges from a very common problem in technology development. Technical teams are often asked to build systems that are not only efficient, reliable, and safe, but also ethical. Yet when engineers, programmers, designers, or project managers ask what the ethical answer is, the response they receive is often frustrating. Ethics rarely produces a single neat rule that can simply be coded into a specification. Instead, it raises distinctions, competing considerations, questions about responsibility, and disagreements about what should count as harm, benefit, fairness, dignity, or care.

That does not mean ethical reflection is vague or useless. On the contrary, it means ethics performs a different function. In many real-world cases, the task is not to discover one universally uncontested answer, but to improve the quality of deliberation around a decision. Ethical reasoning helps identify what is at stake, who may be affected, which values are in tension, what assumptions are hidden in a proposal, and where a design may be under-justified. It clarifies the landscape in which decisions are made. EeVA has been built with precisely this more modest, but more realistic, understanding of ethics in mind. It is a tool for reflection, not an oracle.

Why multiple ethical frameworks matter

One reason ethical deliberation can be difficult is that moral reasoning is not based on only one starting point. Different ethical traditions attend to different features of a problem. A utilitarian analysis may ask about consequences, harms, and benefits across populations. A deontological approach may ask whether a proposal respects duties, rights, and moral constraints that should not be violated even for good outcomes. Virtue ethics may focus on character, practical wisdom, and what kind of professional or institutional habits a decision encourages. Care ethics may draw attention to dependence, vulnerability, relationships, and the needs of those who are easily overlooked. Contractualist approaches may ask whether a policy or action could be justified to others under conditions of reasonable disagreement.

These frameworks do not always converge. The same proposal may appear compelling from one perspective and deeply troubling from another. That is not a flaw to be eliminated. It is often the very reason ethical reflection is needed. EeVA therefore evaluates a use case across multiple frameworks rather than collapsing everything into one score or one final verdict. Its purpose is to help users see where a proposal appears strong, where it appears weak, and where further justification or revision may be needed.

The role of AI in EeVA

EeVA uses large language models as a scaffolding technology for rapid ethical analysis. This brings clear advantages. LLMs can summarise, compare, explain, and generate different lines of moral reasoning quickly. They can help non-specialists engage questions that would otherwise require significant time, multiple experts, or extensive prior reading in moral philosophy. For operational teams working under pressure, this can make ethical reflection more accessible and more routine.

At the same time, EeVA does not treat AI-generated output as authoritative in itself. Language models can reproduce bias, overstate confidence, flatten disagreement, and present uncertain reasoning in highly polished prose. For that reason, EeVA is built around a comparative and assistive role for AI. The agent is intended to support human users in thinking more carefully, not to remove their responsibility. The output should be read as a structured prompt for reflection and discussion, not as a substitute for ethical judgement, institutional review, or professional accountability.

What EeVA is for

In practical terms, EeVA is meant to help users submit a use case, generate a multi-framework evaluation, and retrieve the resulting analysis through a simple interface. Behind that interface sits an n8n-based workflow that receives the uploaded material, assigns a run identifier, processes the text, produces framework-specific evaluations, and then generates a synthesis that can be retrieved later. The point of the system, however, is not merely technical efficiency. The deeper aim is to offer a usable structure through which ethically untrained or time-limited teams can slow down just enough to think better.

EeVA should therefore be understood as a research-led ethical reflection tool. It is intended to broaden moral visibility rather than close debate too early. A good EeVA output does not end the conversation; it improves it. If it helps a team recognise overlooked stakeholders, sharpen a justification, reconsider a design decision, or see that a proposal looks different from another moral standpoint, then it has already served its purpose.
