ABOUT EeVA

Ethical Deliberation Without Solving Ethics

EeVA (the Ethical eValuation Agent) is designed as a practical aid for ethical reflection. It does not claim to produce one correct answer to moral problems. Instead, it helps users examine a proposed use-case through several ethical lenses so that assumptions, tensions, trade-offs, and unanswered questions become clearer.

EeVA emerges from a very common problem: Non-ethically trained personnel are often asked to solve problems that are ethically laden. Yet when engineers, programmers, designers, or even social workers, policy developers, or healthcare professionals ask what an ethical answer may be, the response they receive is often frustrating. Ethicists rarely produce a single neat rule that can simply be coded into a specification, or applied without question to a policy. Instead, ethicists raise distinctions, competing considerations, questions about responsibility, and disagreements about what should count as harm, benefit, fairness, dignity, or care.

That does not mean ethical reflection is vague or useless. On the contrary, it means ethics performs a different function. In many real-world cases, the purpose of ethical deliberation is not to discover one universally uncontested answer, but to improve the ethical quality of decisions. Ethical reasoning helps identify what is at stake, who may be affected, which values are in tension, what assumptions are hidden, and where a design may be under-justified. It clarifies the landscape in which decisions are made. EeVA has been built with precisely this more modest, but more realistic, understanding of ethics in mind. It is a tool for reflection, not an oracle.

Why Multiple Ethical Frameworks Matter

One reason ethical deliberation can be frustrating for non-ethically trained professionals is that moral reasoning is not based on a single starting point. Different ethical traditions hold to different moral foundations. A utilitarian analysis may focus on consequences, harms, and benefits across populations. A deontological approach may look to whether a proposal respects duties, rights, and moral constraints that should not be violated even for good outcomes. Virtue ethics may focus on character, practical wisdom, and what kind of professional or institutional habits a decision encourages. Care ethics may draw attention to dependence, vulnerability, relationships, and the needs of those who are easily overlooked. Contractualist approaches may ask whether a policy or action could be justified to others under conditions of reasonable disagreement.

These frameworks do not always converge. The same ethical proposal may appear compelling from one perspective and deeply troubling from another. That is not a flaw to be eliminated. It is often the very reason ethical reflection is needed. EeVA, therefore, evaluates a use-case across multiple frameworks rather than collapsing everything into one score or one final verdict. Its purpose is to help users see where a proposal converges with an ethical framework, where it diverges, and where further justification or revision may be needed.

The Role of AI in EeVA

EeVA uses large language models as a scaffolding technology for rapid ethical analysis. This brings clear advantages. LLMs can summarise, compare, explain, and generate different lines of moral reasoning quickly. They can help non-specialists engage questions that would otherwise require significant time, multiple experts, or extensive prior reading in moral philosophy. For operational teams working under pressure, this can make ethical reflection more accessible and more routine.

At the same time, EeVA does not treat AI-generated output as authoritative. Language models can reproduce bias, overstate confidence, flatten disagreement, and present uncertain reasoning in highly polished prose. For that reason, EeVA is built as a comparative and assistive tool. The agent is intended to support human users in thinking more carefully, not to remove their responsibility. The output should be read as a structured prompt for reflection and discussion, not as a substitute for ethical judgement, institutional review, or professional accountability.

How Does EeVA Work?

In practical terms, EeVA is meant to help users submit a use-case, generate a multi-framework evaluation, and retrieve the resulting analysis through a simple interface. Behind that interface sits an n8n-based workflow that receives the uploaded material, assigns a run identifier, processes the text, produces framework-specific evaluations, and then generates a synthesis that can be retrieved later. The point of the system, however, is not merely technical efficiency. The deeper aim is to offer a usable structure through which ethically untrained or time-limited teams can slow down just enough to think more about ethical implementations.

EeVA should, therefore, be understood as a research-led ethical reflection tool. It is intended to broaden moral visibility rather than close debate too early. A good EeVA output does not end the conversation; it improves it. If it helps a team recognise overlooked stakeholders, sharpen a justification, reconsider a design decision, or see that a proposal looks different from another moral standpoint, then it has already served its purpose.
