Understanding EeVA's ethical landscape

Ethical Frameworks

EeVA examines a use case through ten different ethical perspectives. Each asks different questions: what happens to people, what duties should be respected, who is treated fairly, and what relationships might change. Their conclusions may disagree. This is useful: no single viewpoint captures every ethical concern.

How to use this page: Select any framework below to read a plain-language explanation and see its scholarly sources. The definitions are stable reference material, not generated as part of your individual evaluation. You do not need to read every framework to understand your results.

01DeontologySome actions must respect moral duties, even when breaking them could produce useful results.+

Deontology asks whether an action is morally acceptable in itself, not merely whether it produces a good outcome. A decision might save money or help many people but still be wrong if it violates an important duty. Examples include duties to tell the truth, keep promises, avoid exploiting people, and respect individuals as persons rather than treating them simply as tools for achieving an outcome. Different deontological theories disagree about which duties matter most, whether exceptions are permitted, and what happens when duties conflict. For an engineer, this approach might ask whether a system deceives users, makes decisions without proper consent, or unfairly sacrifices the interests of one person for benefits enjoyed by others. EeVA therefore examines the rules and obligations involved, whose rights and agency are at stake, and whether efficiency or convenience is being used to excuse something that should not be done. Deontology does not mean that consequences never matter; it means that consequences alone are not always sufficient to justify an action.

02Classical UtilitarianismChoose the option expected to produce the greatest overall well-being, giving everyone's interests equal weight.+

Classical utilitarianism evaluates decisions by their likely effects on happiness, suffering, or overall well-being. A morally preferable option is one that produces more overall good, or less overall harm, than its realistic alternatives. Importantly, the interests of all those affected count, not merely the interests of the designer, company, or most influential users. Suppose a transport algorithm reduces average travel time but creates serious disadvantages for people with disabilities. A utilitarian analysis would consider those disadvantages alongside the broader benefits, rather than assuming that a higher average performance makes the design ethical. EeVA uses this framework to examine who benefits, who bears costs, how strong the expected effects are, and whether a better alternative exists. Difficulties arise when benefits and harms are hard to measure, when effects are uncertain, or when large gains for many seem to justify severe losses for a few. Classical utilitarianism is one influential version of outcome-based ethics, not a synonym for every form of consequentialism.

Definition sources: Based on the utilitarian material used in EeVA, including Joe Slater's historical overview and Stephen Nathanson's discussion of act and rule utilitarianism in the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy.

Further reading: History of Utilitarianism — Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy ↗

03Rawlsian JusticeDesign fair rules that people could accept without knowing whether they would be advantaged or disadvantaged.+

Rawlsian justice is associated with philosopher John Rawls and his idea of justice as fairness. Imagine choosing the basic rules of a society without knowing whether you would be rich or poor, healthy or ill, influential or disadvantaged. Rawls calls this a way of reasoning behind a “veil of ignorance.” Because you cannot arrange the rules to favour your own position, you have a reason to protect equal basic freedoms and take the situation of less advantaged people seriously. This is not simply the claim that everyone must receive an identical outcome. It concerns fair institutions, genuine opportunities, and the justification of inequalities. For a technological use case, EeVA might ask whether access to a service depends on wealth, whether the system protects basic freedoms, and whether its design can be justified to people who end up worst off. Rawls developed his theory mainly for the basic structure of society, so applying it to a single product or algorithm requires care. The framework is especially useful where technologies distribute opportunities, resources, risks or social power.

Definition sources: Based on EeVA's Rawlsian Justice overview, primarily drawing on Leif Wenar's Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on John Rawls.

Further reading: John Rawls — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy ↗

04Rights-Based EthicsAsk which entitlements people have, and what others must do to respect and protect them.+

Rights-based ethics begins with the idea that people have claims that should not simply be ignored whenever doing so would be convenient. Rights can protect freedoms, such as expression or privacy, and may also support claims to fair treatment or necessary protections. Rights also create responsibilities: if a person has a right to something, another person or institution may have a duty not to interfere with it, or in some cases to help secure it. In engineering, this raises questions about privacy, informed agreement, discrimination, access, safety and the possibility of challenging decisions. For example, an automated decision system might be accurate on average while still violating a person's privacy or denying them an opportunity to contest a decision. EeVA asks which rights are relevant, who holds them, who must respect them, and whether limitations can be justified. Not every desirable benefit is a right; different rights can also conflict. The key challenge is to explain which rights apply in the particular use case and what they require in practice.

Definition sources: EeVA's rights-based overview was developed as a synthesis drawing on the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy and Encyclopedia.com; the summary here is independently written.

Further reading: Rights — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy ↗

05ContractualismCould the rules behind this decision be justified to each person affected by them?+

Contractualism asks whether we could justify our actions to other people on terms they could reasonably accept. A widely used version, associated with T. M. Scanlon, considers whether a general rule could be reasonably rejected by someone who would have to live under it. This is not the same as requiring everyone to sign a literal contract, nor does it mean that any personal dislike automatically defeats a rule. Reasons for rejection must be considered fairly alongside the reasons of other people. Imagine a digital service that increases convenience for most users but excludes a small group who cannot use smartphones. A contractualist analysis would ask whether the exclusion could be justified to those people, and whether an alternative rule would impose fewer or more acceptable burdens. EeVA therefore looks at people's individual complaints, the fairness of proposed rules, and the reasons different stakeholders could offer one another. Contractualism is especially useful when an aggregate benefit hides a serious burden on an identifiable person or group.

Definition sources: Based on EeVA's contractualist material, principally drawing on Elizabeth Ashford and Tim Mulgan's Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry.

Further reading: Contractualism — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy ↗

06PrinciplismExamine autonomy, doing good, avoiding harm, and justice together.+

Principlism offers a practical way to examine ethical problems using a small set of widely recognised principles. The best-known approach comes from medical ethics and uses four: respect for autonomy (people's ability to make informed choices), beneficence (promoting well-being), non-maleficence (avoiding harm), and justice (treating people fairly and distributing benefits and burdens appropriately). These principles do not always point in the same direction. For instance, a safety system may reduce danger while restricting users' freedom to choose. A principlist evaluation makes both concerns explicit rather than assuming that one automatically settles the case. EeVA applies this approach by identifying which principles are supported, which are threatened, and where the use case needs better safeguards or a clearer justification for trade-offs. Principlism is particularly useful for making ethical discussions accessible to teams without specialist philosophical training. Its limitation is that naming principles does not, by itself, explain precisely how to resolve conflicts between them. That requires careful attention to the actual situation and reasons for the choices made.

Definition sources: Based on EeVA's principlism source, Donald C. Ainslie's Encyclopedia.com entry, and the Beauchamp–Childress approach it discusses.

Further reading: Principlism — Encyclopedia.com ↗

07Virtue EthicsAsk what a good, responsible person or organisation would do, and what habits the technology encourages.+

Virtue ethics focuses on moral character rather than only on rules or measurable outcomes. It asks what qualities a good decision-maker should develop and demonstrate, such as honesty, fairness, courage, care, sound judgment and practical wisdom. Practical wisdom means recognising which considerations matter in a real situation and responding appropriately, rather than mechanically following a formula. For example, a company might meet the minimum legal requirements for an AI product but still act irresponsibly if it conceals known limitations or ignores warnings from affected users. Virtue ethics would examine the judgment, integrity and sense of responsibility reflected in those decisions. EeVA can also ask what kinds of behaviour a technology encourages in its designers, operators and users. Does it support trustworthiness, fairness and attentiveness, or does it reward manipulation and indifference? One difficulty is that different communities may describe virtues differently, and admirable character alone does not determine the correct technical design. Nevertheless, the framework makes moral responsibility and good judgment visible where checklists and metrics cannot capture them.

Definition sources: Based on EeVA's virtue ethics overview, drawing on Rosalind Hursthouse and Glen Pettigrove's Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry.

Further reading: Virtue Ethics — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy ↗

08Care EthicsPay particular attention to relationships, vulnerability, dependence and the practical realities of caring.+

Care ethics starts from a basic observation: people do not live as completely independent individuals. We depend on family members, colleagues, professionals, public services and one another throughout our lives. These relationships create moral responsibilities, especially where people are vulnerable or cannot easily protect their own interests. Care ethics therefore asks not only whether a rule is fair or whether an outcome is efficient, but also whether particular people are being listened to, understood and supported. Consider replacing a human service with an automated chatbot. Even if the chatbot gives accurate information, it may fail people who need reassurance, personal attention, or help navigating complicated circumstances. EeVA uses care ethics to consider the quality of relationships, unequal dependence, responsibility for follow-up, and the lived experiences of those affected. Care ethics does not mean that personal feelings always override general rules. Rather, it highlights aspects of moral life that abstract rules and average outcome measures may overlook, particularly the importance of responding well to actual people in their specific circumstances.

Definition sources: Based on EeVA's ethics of care material, drawing on Maureen Sander-Staudt's Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry.

Further reading: Care Ethics — Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy ↗

09Communitarian EthicsConsider how decisions affect shared practices, belonging, trust and the communities people rely on.+

Communitarian ethics places greater emphasis on the communities and relationships through which people develop their identities, values and responsibilities. Individuals make choices, but those choices take place within families, workplaces, neighbourhoods, professional groups and wider social institutions. Decisions can therefore affect more than personal benefits and harms: they may strengthen or weaken trust, shared responsibility, social cooperation and a sense of belonging. An automated public service, for example, might be efficient for individual transactions while making it harder for a local community to support people who need face-to-face help. EeVA uses this perspective to examine effects on shared institutions, social relationships, community participation and the values a group considers important. Communitarian approaches vary, and they should not be read as an instruction to preserve every existing practice. Some communities exclude or disadvantage people; community preferences must still face ethical criticism. The question is how technology can serve people as members of interconnected communities, rather than treating every decision as an isolated transaction between an individual and a system.

Definition sources: Based on EeVA's communitarian ethics overview, drawing on Daniel A. Bell's Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry.

Further reading: Communitarianism — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy ↗

10CasuistryReason from well-understood practical cases to identify what matters in a new case.+

Casuistry is a way of making ethical judgments by carefully comparing a difficult situation with other cases whose moral features are better understood. Instead of beginning with one general rule and mechanically applying it, the analyst asks what is similar, what is different, and which differences change the ethical judgment. In medicine, for example, a new decision about sharing patient information might be compared with familiar situations involving consent, confidentiality and immediate danger. In engineering, a new automated decision system might be compared with established practices where human accountability, safety and fairness are already debated. EeVA uses casuistry to examine important details of the proposed use case and to test whether seemingly small changes lead to a different moral assessment. Its strength is attention to the practical circumstances that abstract theories can miss. Its limitation is that cases are not automatically comparable and past practice may itself have been unjust. Good case-based reasoning must therefore explain why a particular comparison is relevant and remain open to criticism.

Definition sources: Based on EeVA's casuistry material, drawing on A. Garcia and D. Monlezun's contribution to the Encyclopedia of Global Bioethics (Springer, 2016).

Further reading: Casuistry — Encyclopedia of Global Bioethics ↗
